# What should a future software engineer or an applied researcher focus on?

---

Given rapid advances in LLMs, AI agents, and loop engineering, the critical skills for future software engineers and applied researchers will shift from executing individual tasks toward the following capabilities.

1. Build deep fundamentals and domain expertise.
Understand enough to reason independently, recognize bad assumptions, and know when AI is wrong.

2. Become exceptional at verification.
Design tests, metrics, experiments, invariants, simulations, and evidence that establish whether work is actually correct.

3. Design reliable loops.
Specify goals, context, tools, memory/state, verification, recovery, budgets, and stopping conditions.

4. Progress from task ownership to problem ownership, and build the taste to choose well.
Don't merely execute assigned work. Understand the domain deeply enough to identify what should be solved next, and learn to distinguish technically possible work from important work, promising hypotheses from noise, and meaningful results from misleading ones.

  Become a philosopher, or a part-time one. Take a step back and ask, "Does solving this problem really matter?"
  
  As routine coding, data analysis, system design, and applied research become increasingly automated, juniors can no longer rely on sheer volume of hands-on implementation to build this judgment. Build it instead by manually breaking down AI-generated failures, running post-mortems on edge cases, and testing agent decisions against real-world domain constraints.

5. Design organizations of agents, and allocate their intelligence well.
Don't directly manage thousands of agents one by one. Decompose difficult problems, coordinate specialized AI workers, aggregate evidence, resolve disagreements, and maintain quality at scale — and as execution becomes cheap, decide where that execution is actually worth spending: which agent-hours, experiments, and compute deserve the investment.

6. Polish communication skills.
Human-human communication and human-AI system communication will become more critical as execution becomes cheap and fast.

We should stop measuring juniors primarily by experiments run, code shipped, or papers implemented, and start deliberately training "problem → loop → evidence → decision" ownership.

Preserve your own learning loop. Use AI aggressively, but don't outsource understanding and critical thinking. Predict before asking, inspect failures, explain solutions from first principles, and occasionally solve important problems yourself.
